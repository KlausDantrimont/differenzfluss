# The Lens Shop

## An Epistemic Intermediate Language for AI Systems

Klaus Dantrimont 2026

Artificial intelligence can produce remarkably good answers.

It can analyze, compare, explain, generate hypotheses, develop scenarios, and identify contradictions. Yet something remains strangely invisible in many of these achievements:

**From which perspective was the thinking actually done?**

An AI can examine the same problem psychologically, technically, historically, institutionally, causally, or normatively. It can treat it as a feedback system. It can ask about actors or structures, states or processes, evidence or meaning.

Most of the time, it makes these choices implicitly.

The user sees the answer.

The choice of perspective remains in the background.

Perhaps there is an underestimated interface here.

Not between human and machine.

But between **the object and the act of thinking about it**.

---

## Before the Answer Comes the Question

Every analysis begins with a selection.

What are we looking at?

Which differences are relevant?

Which relations matter?

Which temporal scale do we choose?

Are we looking for causes?

Functions?

Interests?

States?

Transitions?

Rules?

Power?

Evidence?

The object itself does not answer these questions.

Rather, they determine **what kind of object becomes visible to us in the first place**.

A school introduces a new rule. The number of reported conflicts increases.

We can ask:

What effect does the rule have?

How does behavior change over time?

What is actually being reported as a conflict?

Which institutional structures are emerging?

How do students, teachers, and parents experience the same development?

Each of these questions cuts through the same situation differently.

None of them has to be wrong.

But none of them is the situation itself.

---

## Epistemic Operators

The Lens Shop begins with a simple idea:

Perhaps many such perspectival moves can be reduced to relatively elementary epistemic operations.

For example:

**TIME**
How does something change?

**STATE**
What configuration currently exists?

**RELATION**
What is connected to what?

**CAUSALITY**
What causes what?

**PERSPECTIVE**
How does the picture change with the observer?

**INFORMATION**
Who knows what, when, and through which channels?

**EVIDENCE**
What supports a claim?

**INSTITUTION**
Which structures reproduce themselves over time?

**INCENTIVE**
Which consequences alter behavior?

**SELECTION**
Which variants persist, and which disappear?

**SCALE**
What changes when we change the level of observation?

**FEEDBACK**
Which effects feed back into their own causes?

These concepts are not a theory of **what the world is**.

They are operations concerning **how the world can be examined**.

That distinction matters.

The Lens Shop does not claim that reality consists of time, relation, institution, selection, or perspective.

It merely says:

These cuts can generate knowledge.

---

## Lenses Are Compositions

A single operator does not yet constitute a complete perspective.

Things become interesting when operators are combined.

For an intermittent problem in a software system, for example, four operators might be particularly useful:

**TIME · STATE · RELATION · INFORMATION**

The temporal dimension reveals that the disturbance is not permanent.

The state operator draws attention to the fact that a restart apparently resets something.

RELATION prevents us from examining individual components only in isolation.

INFORMATION asks whether the available metrics capture the states that are actually relevant to the disturbance.

Four relatively simple cuts produce a specific analytical perspective.

Another situation requires another combination.

That is the actual Lens Shop:

Not a shelf full of fixed worldviews.

But a **construction kit for perspectives**.

---

## As Little as Possible

The principle is explicitly not:

> The more perspectives, the better.

Every additional perspective costs attention and complexity.

If twenty operators are applied simultaneously to a small problem, the result may not be deeper analysis.

It may simply be more text.

A good lens should therefore be as small as possible.

The principle is:

> **As little epistemic structure as possible, as much as necessary for good orientation.**

This requires an epistemic budget.

For every additional operator, one can ask:

What new cut does it introduce?

Is that cut genuinely orthogonal to the ones already in use?

Or does it merely describe almost the same thing again?

Which blind spot does it close?

How large is the expected gain in insight?

What is the cost in additional complexity?

And eventually:

**Is this enough?**

Stopping an analysis is itself an epistemic operation.

---

## Dynamic Lens Construction

This allows an AI to do more than apply a predefined perspective.

It can construct the perspective itself.

Given a situation, problem, or irritation, the AI first asks:

**What exactly requires explanation here?**

It then selects from the operator catalogue a minimal set of cuts with high expected epistemic value.

It explains its selection.

It also names plausible operators that it deliberately does not use yet.

Then it applies the resulting perspective.

Afterwards, it examines the remaining problem:

What has been explained well?

What remains open?

Which blind spots are produced by the chosen lens itself?

Is another cut required?

Or would further analysis without new information merely produce decorative complexity?

Perspective selection thus becomes a controllable process.

The AI does not merely analyze an object.

It also analyzes **its own analytical architecture**.

---

## The Inverse Operation

At this point, a second possibility appears.

If a perspective can be constructed from operators, perhaps the process can also be reversed.

Suppose we are given not a situation, but a report.

A narrative.

A political speech.

A scientific paper.

A strategy document.

A personal account of a conflict.

The question is no longer:

**Which lens should we use?**

It becomes:

> **Which lens is already being used here?**

Which epistemic cuts structure the narrative?

Which kinds of relations count as explanatory?

What becomes visible?

What recedes into the background?

A text might, for example, be structured mainly by:

**CAUSALITY · INCENTIVE · ROLE**

People then appear primarily as acting agents, decisions as causes, and behavior as a response to consequences.

Another report about the same events might mainly use:

**TIME · INSTITUTION · FEEDBACK**

Individual actors suddenly become less important.

Long-term structures and self-stabilizing processes move into view.

Both reports may concern the same object.

And yet they construct epistemically different worlds.

---

## Epistemic Factorization

This reverse operation can be understood as **epistemic factorization**.

The aim is to reduce a complex perspective to the smallest plausible set of supporting operators.

Not:

Which operators can somehow be found in the text?

But:

> **Which minimal combination explains the characteristic way this account sees its subject?**

The factorization need not be unique.

A text may combine several perspectives.

Operators may partially overlap.

Some assignments may remain uncertain.

For precisely this reason, the reconstruction itself needs tests.

What happens if a suspected operator is removed?

Does the text lose an important part of its structure?

Could another operator explain the same movement better?

Are two selected operators genuinely different, or merely two labels for almost the same cut?

The result is not a psychological profile of the author.

It does not say:

**This is how this person thinks.**

It says:

**This is how thinking is organized in this text.**

---

## Visibility and Blind Spots

A further application follows almost automatically.

Every lens creates visibility.

And every lens creates blind spots.

That is not initially a criticism.

No report can represent everything at once.

The interesting question is:

Which operators that would be plausible for the same subject are missing?

A conflict may be explained entirely through individual intentions.

Institutional or historical cuts may then be absent.

An economic report may focus only on incentives and metrics.

Power, meaning, or long-term feedback may be missing.

A moral account may work primarily with NORM and ROLE.

The question of evidence may remain weak.

This does not immediately imply that the report is manipulative or false.

A more neutral and often more precise statement is:

> **This representation creates these forms of visibility and these blind spots.**

Only afterwards do questions of one-sidedness, truth, bias, or strategic selection arise.

---

## Not a Truth Machine

The Lens Shop does not decide what is true.

That is an important boundary.

A beautifully constructed causal perspective can be based on false data.

A perfectly factorized narrative may contain untrue claims.

A multiperspective analysis can still be wrong.

Epistemic structure and truth are not the same thing.

The Lens Shop operates one level earlier.

It makes visible:

**which questions are being asked,**

**which cuts are being used,**

**which kinds of explanation are being produced,**

and

**which alternatives remain invisible at first.**

Evidence assessment, research, experimentation, statistics, or argumentation can follow afterwards.

The Lens Shop does not replace these procedures.

It can help determine **which of them are needed in the first place**.

---

## An Intermediate Language

This allows the concept to be located more precisely.

The Lens Shop is not a prompt catalogue.

It is not a collection of expert roles.

It is not a multi-agent system.

It is not an ontology.

It is not a complete epistemology.

It is better understood as:

> **an epistemic intermediate language for AI systems.**

Its vocabulary consists of elementary epistemic operators.

Its grammar consists of their composition.

Its optimization principle is the epistemic budget.

Its forward operation is the construction of a perspective.

Its inverse operation is the factorization of a perspective.

Its purpose is orientation.

Or, more technically:

> **A compositional operator catalogue for constructing and reconstructing analytical perspectives.**

That sounds more imposing than a Lens Shop.

It means the same thing.

---

## Between User and AI

The most immediate application lies in ordinary AI sessions.

Today, users often assign roles to AI systems:

> Be a critical analyst.

> Think like a historian.

> Examine this as a psychologist.

Such instructions work surprisingly well.

But epistemically, they are vague.

What exactly makes a “critical analyst” critical?

Which cognitive operations should be preferred?

Which should be avoided?

Operators allow a session to be configured more explicitly.

For example:

> Examine the problem initially using
> TIME + RELATION + COUNTER-HYPOTHESIS.
> Use CAUSALITY only once sufficient evidence is available.

Or:

> First reconstruct the dominant perspective of the report.
> Then introduce exactly one underrepresented operator as a countercheck.

The AI is no longer given merely a role.

It is given an **epistemic mode of operation**.

---

## Between Application and Reasoning System

The same idea can be embedded more deeply into AI systems.

One can imagine an architecture:

**User or application**

↓

Problem
Report
Decision
Research question

↓

**Epistemic intermediate layer**

Operators
Lens construction
Factorization
Blind-spot analysis
Epistemic budget

↓

**Reasoning system**

Analysis
Research
Agents
Hypothesis generation
Simulation
Synthesis

The operator layer does not itself decide content.

It structures the process of inquiry.

This potentially makes it independent of any particular language model.

Different models can receive the same specification.

They will probably use it differently.

That is precisely what makes the idea interesting.

The operators do not define the result.

They define the **space of allowed or preferred epistemic moves**.

---

## Multiple Agents, Multiple Lenses

Multi-agent systems can also be structured in this way.

Today, agents are often designed as characters:

the optimist,
the skeptic,
the pragmatist,
the expert.

This is intuitive.

But agents could also be defined epistemically.

Agent A examines:

**TIME · PROCESS · FEEDBACK**

Agent B:

**INCENTIVE · ROLE · POWER**

Agent C:

**EVIDENCE · COUNTER-HYPOTHESIS · CAUSALITY**

The agents then differ not primarily in personality.

They differ in their **analytical operations**.

This could make conflicts between agents more precise.

We would know not merely that they disagree.

We could reconstruct **why they see different things**.

---

## Research and Analysis

Another application lies in research processes.

A research question can be epistemically profiled in advance:

Which operators are we already using?

Which are absent?

Are supposedly competing theories perhaps not direct competitors at all, because they use different cuts?

Is a dispute causal, normative, conceptual, or perspectival?

Literature reviews could also be read through factorization.

Not only:

Who claims what?

But:

**Which epistemic operations dominate a field of research?**

Perhaps a subject has been examined almost exclusively in causal terms for decades.

Or exclusively at the level of individuals.

A change of scale or an institutional cut might then do more than produce a new answer.

It might open an entirely new **class of questions**.

---

## Journalism, Politics, and Public Narratives

Public texts can be examined in the same way.

A political narrative may be strongly normative and actor-centered.

Another may be institutional and historical.

A third may be economic and causal.

Instead of immediately asking:

**Who is right?**

one can first ask:

**What kind of world does this representation produce?**

This is particularly useful when two sides appear to discuss the same thing but are actually operating on different epistemic levels.

Sometimes their answers contradict each other.

Sometimes they are simply asking different questions.

That distinction will not resolve conflicts.

But it can locate them more precisely.

---

## Education

For educational use, the operator catalogue could be radically reduced.

Students would not need to learn an epistemic algebra.

A small set of basic questions might be enough:

What is changing?

What is related to what?

From whose perspective?

How do we know?

Which rules are operating?

What else could explain this?

The Lens Shop would then become a grammar of questioning.

Not:

**Which answer should I believe?**

But:

**Which question is still missing?**

This may become particularly valuable when working with AI.

A machine capable of producing fluent answers at any moment increases the value of good questions.

---

## Limits of the Approach

The Lens Shop itself requires criticism.

There is no reason to assume that its operator catalogue is complete.

Some operators may be missing.

Some may be redundant.

Others may need to be split.

INFORMATION and EVIDENCE, for example, lie close to one another but ask different questions.

The same is true of NORM and INSTITUTION.

Such boundaries often become visible only through use.

That is not an unfortunate side effect.

It is part of the project.

If the operators are genuinely elementary, they must prove useful across very different domains.

A good decomposition should be neither arbitrarily fine nor unnecessarily coarse.

A final factorization may never be achievable.

But it can be improved.

---

## Different Models Will Choose Differently

Different AI systems will also factorize the same situation differently.

That is to be expected.

One model may consider SELECTION central.

Another may prefer CAUSALITY.

A third may begin with SCALE.

This does not necessarily indicate a failure of the method.

Lens construction itself involves judgment.

The important questions are therefore not whether every model chooses the same operators.

More interesting is whether it can:

justify its selection,

recognize redundancy,

identify blind spots,

notice after a first cut that another is required,

and also know when to stop.

These differences between models could themselves become objects of epistemic analysis.

---

## Building Lenses and Recognizing Lenses

A striking symmetry now appears.

Forward:

**operators → perspective**

Backward:

**perspective → operators**

Or, in more detail:

**situation → irritation → operator selection → lens → analysis**

and:

**narrative → representational structure → operators → epistemic profile**

The first movement creates a way of seeing.

The second reconstructs one that already exists.

Together they form a small language for perspectives.

Not for the world itself.

But for the ways in which we cut through it.

---

## What Is This For?

Perhaps part of intelligent orientation consists not only in being able to generate answers.

Perhaps it also consists in noticing:

**Which question am I asking right now?**

**Why this one?**

**Which other question would be possible?**

**What does my perspective make visible?**

**What is it inherently bad at seeing?**

Humans occasionally do this.

AI systems can help.

But to do so, they need a language in which perspectives themselves can become explicit objects of reasoning.

The Lens Shop is an attempt to provide such a language.

Small enough to be practical.

Abstract enough to work across domains.

Compositional enough to generate new perspectives.

And open enough to revise itself.

Perhaps an intelligent machine does not always need to wear the same lens.

Perhaps it does not need to wear as many as possible at once either.

Perhaps it is enough, at first, to be able

**to know that it is wearing one,**

**to describe it,**

**to construct a more suitable one,**

and

**to put it down again once it has served its purpose.**

That would already be quite a lot.
